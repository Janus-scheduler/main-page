About the project

A scheduling component we needed and could not find

Janus began as a coursework problem: a rostering tool for a teaching hospital, four weeks, no budget for a commercial calendar. Every open-source option was tied to one framework, every good one wanted a licence fee, and all of them assumed a server we did not have. We wrote a throwaway timeline instead, and the throwaway kept being asked for.

Two years later it is a final-year project with a versioned API, a benchmark suite and a public changelog. The scope has stayed narrow on purpose: Janus renders and manipulates a schedule, correctly and quickly, and leaves storage, permissions and business rules to the application around it. Where we have chosen not to build something, the documentation says so plainly.

Why "Janus"

Janus is the Roman god of doorways and beginnings, shown with two faces looking in opposite directions — one to what has been, one to what is coming.

A calendar does the same thing: it holds a record and a plan in one view. The name also happens to describe the architecture — one core facing your data, one facing the DOM.

What we set out to prove

four project goals · assessed against the report
Goal 01 Framework independence

One rendering core, consumed identically from four frameworks and from plain JavaScript.

Goal 02 No backend required

Everything — including parsing and conflict detection — runs in the browser, offline.

Goal 03 Correct time handling

UTC storage, zoned display, and a test suite that crosses daylight-saving boundaries.

Goal 04 Honest scope

Every limitation documented where a developer will meet it, not buried in an issue.

Academic report

preprint · 68 pages · updated 12 Aug 2026
Abstract

Scheduling interfaces on the web are usually delivered as framework-specific components, which forces a rewrite whenever the host application changes stack, and commonly assume a server for conflict resolution and natural-language input. This work presents Janus Scheduler, a framework-agnostic scheduling component library built on custom elements, with a headless core that performs range algebra, recurrence expansion and timezone-aware conflict detection entirely on the client.

We further show that useful natural-language event entry does not require a large language model: a quantised static embedding table with a nearest-neighbour classifier achieves 96.4% intent accuracy and 0.93 entity F1 on a corpus of 1,000 British-English scheduling utterances, at a median parse cost of 0.8 ms and a 38 kB artefact. Evaluation against four commercial and three open-source alternatives shows a 94% reduction in first-paint time at ten thousand events.
